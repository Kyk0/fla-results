Teachers for hard against soft floor

Before a camera can learn from the sole, the sole has to label the steps. Three teachers label every footstep of the Booster K1 as hard or soft floor after the step: one reads only the sole, one only the joints and IMU, one both. Every step is labelled by a model that never trained on its episode.

Simulation only. One gait, two floor classes.

In short

  1. The task is almost solved by every teacher. Sole 0.997, joints and IMU 0.990, both together 0.995, on 22,773 steps. Chance is 0.500.
  2. The sole is slightly better than the joints. +0.007 on all steps, real by the rule used here, but both error rates are already below 1 %.
  3. Reading both does not help over the sole alone.
  4. Standing and stopping is the only weak spot. On steps commanded below 0.05 m/s, the joints teacher drops to 0.899, the sole to 0.975.
  5. The consequence: the three label sets agree with truth on 99.0 to 99.7 % of steps, so a vision head trained on any of them cannot show which teacher is better on this task.

What was done

  • Data. Every walking episode of G113, an ordinary flat-foot walking policy, in the step-1 data on hard floor (240), soft floor (240), and strips that start on hard floor and walk onto soft (60): 22,805 steps. 22,773 are scored; 32 transition steps have no class and are labelled but not scored.
  • A step is a touchdown before any fall, with its stance window from touchdown to lift-off, 5 to 30 frames at 50 Hz. Truth is the floor class under the landing foot.
  • Teachers. The small temporal convolutional network of the step-1 work, on one step's stance window. The sole teacher reads only 16 coarse sole features: four pressure blocks, four shear vectors, centre of pressure, total force and contact area. The joints teacher reads joint positions and velocities and the IMU. The third reads both.
  • Cross-fitting. Five folds by episode. The fold-k model trains on training episodes outside fold k and stops early on validation episodes outside fold k; it then labels the episodes of fold k. No model ever sees a test-split episode, and those hold stiffness and friction ranges kept out of training. Three seeds.
  • Settings were chosen on the validation split only: sole learning rate 0.002, dropout 0.1; joints and both 0.004 and 0.3; width 96 for all. A sole teacher trained on shuffled labels is the control.
  • Chance. Soft floors are 0.462 of the scored steps. A random guess at the class prior scores 0.500, a constant majority answer 0.350.

What it means and its limits

  • An easy task. Hard floor (stiffness 7.7e8) and soft floor (1e4 to 1e5) differ by about four orders of magnitude. In the six-class experiment, by contrast, the hard grippy floor alone scored 0.76.
  • Consequence for vision. The sole, joints and both-together label sets agree with truth on 99.0 to 99.7 % of steps. A vision head trained on any of them can differ from one trained on truth by at most about 1 % label noise, so the vision comparison cannot separate the teachers on this task. It did not: see the fixed-patch results.
  • Window end from the simulator. The stance window ends at the noise-free contact signal of the simulator, as in the earlier experiment. A real robot has to detect lift-off itself.
  • Deceptive floors. The teachers read no image, so a deceptive look cannot change their labels; their scores on deceptive episodes depend only on the physics and are on the results page.
  • Independent re-check. It recomputed the main, speed and shuffled scores and the differences from the stored predictions and found them the same, and found no teacher that labelled an episode it had trained on or that had seen a test episode. The floor-change table rests on the original check only.
